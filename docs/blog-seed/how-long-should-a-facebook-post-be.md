A common worry when you write a post is length. Too short feels empty, and too long feels like homework. So how long should a Facebook post be? There is no single right answer, because the best length depends on what the post is trying to do and who is reading.

This guide gives you practical lengths for the most common types of posts, a method for trimming a draft that has grown too long, and a simple way to test what your own audience prefers. The numbers here are our own guidelines from writing and editing posts, not figures published by Facebook.

[[image: person editing text on a laptop with a coffee | Editing a draft post on a laptop]]

## Length is a tool, not a goal

The point of a post is to be understood and to get a response. Length helps or hurts that. A post should be as long as it needs to be to say one thing clearly, and no longer.

Think about the reader. Most people scroll quickly, often on a phone. They decide in a moment whether to stop. Your first line has to earn the second, and each line has to earn the next.

## Practical lengths by post type

| Post type | Suggested length | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Quick update, such as opening hours | 1 to 2 sentences | The reader needs one fact |
| Offer or announcement | 2 to 4 short sentences | State the offer, the date, and what to do |
| Photo or video post | 1 to 3 sentences | The picture does most of the talking |
| Question to start a conversation | 1 to 2 sentences | Keep it easy to answer |
| Helpful tip | 3 to 6 short sentences | Give the tip and one reason it works |
| Story or customer example | A few short paragraphs | Enough to feel real, with a clear point |
| Longer how-to or explanation | Link to a full guide | A post is a preview, not the whole article |

Treat these as starting ranges. Your audience may like longer or shorter posts, and the test below will show you.

## Check the first line

Facebook usually shows only the start of a long post before a "See more" link. Where that cut falls depends on the screen and the device, so do not count on a fixed number of characters. What you can control is the order of your words.

- Put the most important thing first.
- Make the first line useful on its own.
- Never bury the offer, the date, or the question at the bottom of a long post.

## How to shorten a long draft

If a draft feels heavy, edit it in passes.

1. **Name the one point.** Write it in a sentence. If you cannot, the post tries to do too much.
2. **Cut the warm-up.** Delete the opening line that only clears your throat.
3. **Remove repeats.** If you said it twice, keep the better version.
4. **Swap long words for short ones.** "Use" beats "utilize."
5. **Break up big blocks.** Use short paragraphs of one to three sentences.
6. **Move extra detail to a link.** Put the full story on your website or in a longer post.
7. **Read it aloud.** Where you run out of breath, add a full stop.

## When a longer post works

Short is not always better. A longer post can work well when it has a reason.

- **A real story** that holds attention from line to line
- **A clear list** that people can scan
- **A useful explanation** the reader came for
- **An announcement** that needs several details

If you go long, make it easy to read. Use short paragraphs, plain words, and one idea at a time. A long post that looks like a wall of text will lose readers even if every sentence is good.

## Test your own audience

Your readers may differ from anyone else's. Run a gentle test.

1. For two weeks, write posts of your usual length and note the results.
2. For the next two weeks, make similar posts about 30 percent shorter.
3. Compare reach and engagement. Use the same method each time. Our [engagement rate calculator](/tools/engagement-rate-calculator) helps.
4. Keep what worked, and test again in a few months.

Keep the topics and the posting times as similar as you can, or the test will mislead you. A small test points to an answer. It does not prove one.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Padding to hit a number.** Length is not the goal.
- **Cutting the useful part.** Trim the warm-up, not the substance.
- **Hiding the point.** Put the main message early.
- **Writing a wall of text.** Break it into short paragraphs.
- **Ignoring the picture.** With a strong image, fewer words often work better.
- **Copying another Page's length.** Test your own audience.

## Writing for a phone screen

Most people read Facebook on a phone, so picture a small screen when you write. A paragraph that looks short on a laptop can fill an entire phone screen. Two or three lines per paragraph is a good habit, and one line is fine for emphasis.

White space is your friend. Leave a blank line between paragraphs, and keep lists short. If a post has more than five or six lines of text without a break, ask whether it should be two posts, or whether some of it belongs on your website. A reader who sees a dense block of text will often keep scrolling, even if the words are good.

Read your post on your own phone before you publish it. You will notice immediately where it drags, where a line breaks in an awkward spot, and where a sentence says too much. This takes ten seconds and catches more problems than any rule about length.

## A before-and-after example

Here is how an edit can help. Imagine a draft from a bakery: "We are so excited to share that, after months of planning and a lot of hard work from our wonderful team, we will finally be opening our brand new second location on Main Street this Saturday, and we hope that everyone will come along to celebrate with us and try some of our new products."

That is one long sentence, and the important fact, the date and place, is buried in the middle. A tighter version could read: "We are opening a second shop on Main Street this Saturday. Come and celebrate with us, and try our new loaves. Doors open at 8 AM."

The second version is about a third of the length. It leads with the news, gives the place and day, and ends with the time and an invitation. Nothing useful was lost. Every post you write can be improved in the same way: find the main fact, move it to the front, and cut what is only decoration.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is there a character limit for a Facebook post?** Facebook allows long posts, but the limit is not the point. Aim for clarity.

**Do shorter posts get more engagement?** Sometimes, but not always. It depends on your audience and the type of post. Test your own.

**How long should a caption under a photo be?** One to three sentences is a good start. Let the photo carry the story.

**Should I always include a call to action?** Most posts benefit from one clear next step, such as a question or a link.

**Can AI help me shorten a post?** It can suggest edits, but you should read and approve every word. Our guide to [using AI to write Facebook posts](/blog/how-to-use-ai-to-write-facebook-posts) explains how to keep drafts natural.

## Your next step

Take your most recent long post and cut it using the seven passes above. Compare the two versions side by side. Then publish the shorter one, and check how it does against your usual results.
