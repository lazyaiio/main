Some of the people who read your posts cannot see your photos. They may be blind, have low vision, or be using a slow connection that did not load the image. For them, alt text is how the picture reaches them. Alt text is a short written description of an image, read aloud by assistive tools such as screen readers. This guide shows how to write alt text for social media images, with simple rules, examples, and a checklist.

Writing it takes a minute, and it makes your Page easier for everyone to use. The advice here follows widely used accessibility practice. Tools and screens differ, so check whether your platform or scheduler offers a field for it. For the wider skill of writing posts, see our guide on [how to write Facebook posts that people read](/blog/how-to-write-facebook-posts-people-read).

## What alt text is, and what it is not

Alt text is a short description of what an image shows, written for someone who cannot see it. It is not:

- **A caption.** A caption is written for everyone and appears in the post. Alt text is usually hidden and used by assistive tools.
- **A place for keywords.** Stuffing in search terms helps no one.
- **A replacement for the post text.** The important facts still belong in the post.

A good alt text answers one question: what would a person miss if they could not see this image?

## Why it matters for a small business

- **It includes more people.** Everyone who can read your post should be able to understand it.
- **It helps on slow connections.** If an image fails to load, text may appear in its place.
- **It makes you look careful.** Customers notice when a business thinks about them.
- **It can matter legally in some places.** Accessibility rules differ by country and by type of business, so check what applies to you.

We cannot promise that alt text will change your reach or your search position. Its main value is access.

## Where to add it

Many platforms and tools offer a field for alt text, but where it sits differs. Some show it near the image in the post editor, under an option such as "edit" or "accessibility." Some platforms also write a description automatically. Automatic descriptions are often generic and sometimes wrong, so write your own when you can. If your tool has no field, put the key information in the post text.

## Six rules for good alt text

1. **Describe what matters.** Say what is in the image and why it is in your post.
2. **Be brief.** One or two short sentences are usually enough.
3. **Be specific.** "Golden retriever catching a ball" is better than "dog."
4. **Skip "image of" and "photo of."** Assistive tools already say it is an image.
5. **Include any text in the image.** If a sign or a graphic carries words, write them out.
6. **Match the context.** The same photo may need a different description in a different post.

## Examples: weak and better

| Image | Weak alt text | Better alt text |
|---|---|---|
| A loaf of bread | "Bread" | "Round sourdough loaf with a deep golden crust on a wooden board" |
| A team photo | "Our team" | "Five bakers in white aprons smiling behind the shop counter" |
| A sale graphic | "Sale" | "Graphic reading: Spring sale, 20 percent off all plants, Friday to Sunday" |
| A storefront | "Shop front" | "Blue shop front with a green awning and a chalkboard sign on the pavement" |
| A product close-up | "Product" | "Close-up of a hand-stitched leather wallet in dark brown" |
| A before and after | "Before and after" | "Two photos side by side: a cracked phone screen on the left, a repaired one on the right" |

## Alt text for different types of images

### Product photos

Say what the product is, its color, material, and any feature that the buyer needs. Keep the price and the buying instructions in the post.

### People

Describe what people are doing, not personal details. Do not guess a person's age, health, or background. Name a person only if the post does, and only with their agreement.

### Graphics with text

Write out the words in the graphic. If it is long, put the full text in the post, and keep the alt text to a short summary.

### Charts and infographics

Give the main message, such as "Bar chart showing orders doubled from March to June." Put any detailed numbers in the post text.

### Decorative images

If an image adds nothing, such as a divider or a background, you can skip the description, if your tool allows an empty one. Do not skip alt text on pictures that carry meaning.

## How long should it be?

Aim for one or two short sentences. Many tools allow a longer description, but screen reader users prefer concise text. If you need to say more, put it in the post. Our guide on [Facebook captions for photos](/blog/facebook-captions-for-photos) shows how to write the visible words.

## Can AI write my alt text?

A tool can draft a description, and that can save time. The result needs a check, because AI can misidentify objects, invent details, or miss the point of the image. Compare the draft with the picture, correct it, and add what matters to your post. Our article on [AI vs human social media writing](/blog/ai-vs-human-social-media-writing) explains how to divide the work.

## A simple checklist

- Does it say what is in the image?
- Does it say why the image is in this post?
- Is it short and specific?
- Is any text in the image included?
- Did you avoid guessing about people?
- Did you leave out "image of" and keyword stuffing?
- Would someone who could not see the image understand the post?

## Building alt text into your routine

The easiest way to keep up the habit is to write alt text at the same moment you write the caption. When you choose a photo, ask yourself what someone who cannot see it needs to know, and type that in the field before you move on. If you plan a month of posts at once, add the description to your planning table, in a column next to the photo. That way, nothing is left to remember on the day. Review your descriptions every few weeks, and improve the ones that were vague. Over time you will notice that you write better captions too, because describing a picture clearly makes you look at it more closely.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Leaving it blank.** A missing description tells the reader nothing.
- **Repeating the filename.** "IMG_4032" helps nobody.
- **Writing a paragraph.** Keep it short.
- **Adding facts that are not in the image.** Alt text describes what is there.
- **Using the same text for every photo.** Each image needs its own.
- **Relying on automatic descriptions.** They can be wrong.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does alt text help with search?

It may give search engines a little context, but that is not its purpose. Write it for people first.

### Do I need alt text on every image?

On every image that carries meaning, yes. Decorative images are an exception.

### Should I include emoji in alt text?

No. Describe what is shown in words.

### What if my tool has no alt text field?

Put the key points in the post text, and use a tool that supports it when you can. You can also use the free [Facebook post checker](/tools/post-checker) to review your text.

## Your next step

Take your last three photo posts and write alt text for each one, using the six rules. Keep the descriptions in a note, so that you can reuse and improve them. Then make alt text part of your posting routine, together with the caption and the call to action.
