A sale can bring in customers, but only if people understand it in a few seconds. Many sale posts fail for simple reasons. The discount is buried, the end date is missing, or the post shouts so loudly that readers tune it out. This guide shows you how to write a Facebook post for a sale or an offer that is clear, honest, and easy to act on. It includes a structure you can follow, example posts, and the mistakes to avoid.

The advice here is our own, drawn from common practice in small-business writing. Rules about pricing, discounts, and advertising differ by place, so check your local requirements before you publish. For the wider skill of writing posts that people read, see our guide on [how to write Facebook posts that people read](/blog/how-to-write-facebook-posts-people-read).

## Start with the offer, not the excitement

People scroll quickly. They decide in a moment whether a post is worth their time. A sale post earns that moment by stating the offer plainly in the first line.

- **Weak:** "Big news, everyone! Something exciting is coming!"
- **Strong:** "20 percent off all indoor plants this weekend."

The strong version tells the reader what, how much, and when, in one line. Excitement can follow in the next sentence, if it feels natural.

## The five facts every sale post needs

A reader should never have to ask these. Put each one in the post.

| Fact | Why it matters | Example |
|---|---|---|
| What the offer is | The reader needs to know what they get | "Buy one coffee, get a pastry free" |
| What it applies to | Avoids disappointment | "On any pastry under $5" |
| When it starts and ends | Creates a real deadline | "Friday to Sunday only" |
| Any limits | Builds trust | "One per customer" |
| How to claim it | The next step | "Show this post at the counter" |

If you cannot fill in all five, the offer may not be ready to announce.

## A simple structure

Use this four-part shape for most sale posts.

1. **The offer.** State it in the first line.
2. **The detail.** Add what it covers and any limits.
3. **The deadline.** Give the real end date and time.
4. **The step.** Tell people exactly how to claim it.

Add one clear photo of the product or the shop, and keep the whole post short. Our guide on [how long a Facebook post should be](/blog/how-long-should-a-facebook-post-be) suggests two to four short sentences for an offer.

## Example posts

These are examples, not real offers.

**A cafe weekday offer**

> Two coffees for the price of one, Monday to Thursday, 2 to 5 p.m. Valid until the end of the month, one offer per table. Just ask at the counter. Bring a friend.

**A clothing shop clearance**

> Winter coats are 30 percent off while stock lasts. Sizes are limited, and the sale ends on Sunday at 6 p.m. Visit us or send a message to hold your size.

**A salon quiet-day offer**

> Tuesday afternoons are quieter, so a haircut and blow-dry is $10 less this month. Message us to book a slot before the end of the week.

**An online shop launch offer**

> Our new notebooks are here. Order by Friday and get free shipping on orders over $30. Tap the link to see the full range.

Notice that each one states the offer in the first line, gives a deadline, and ends with one next step.

## Be honest with your numbers

Trust is worth more than one sale. Keep your offers clean.

- **Use real deadlines.** Do not say "last chance" and then run the offer again next week.
- **Show a real original price.** If you say "was $50, now $40," the item should truly have sold at $50. Rules on this vary, so check yours.
- **Do not hide conditions.** If the offer needs a minimum spend, say so in the post.
- **Match the photo to the product.** Do not show a premium item if the offer is on a basic one.
- **Honor what you promise.** If you post it, give it.

If you run a competition or giveaway rather than a discount, read our guide on [Facebook giveaway rules for small businesses](/blog/facebook-giveaway-rules-for-small-businesses).

## Choose the right kind of offer

Not every offer has to be a percentage off. Different goals suit different offers.

- **To get new customers in:** a small first-visit benefit.
- **To fill a quiet time:** a limited offer for that day or hour.
- **To clear stock:** a clear discount on named items.
- **To reward regulars:** a thank-you for returning customers.
- **To raise the average order:** free shipping above an amount, or a bundle.

Offers that cost you little and help the customer, such as a free extra or a useful bundle, can work as well as a price cut. Test what suits your customers and your margins.

## Where to put the call to action

End with one clear step. "Show this post at the counter," "Send us a message to book," or "Tap the link to order" are all clear. Avoid piling up several asks. Our guide to [hooks and calls to action](/blog/facebook-post-hooks-and-calls-to-action) lists patterns to try and explains how to avoid sounding pushy.

If you link to a page, open the link on a phone before you publish and check that the offer shows on it.

## When to post it, and how often

Post once when the offer starts, once in the middle, and once near the end. Change the first line and the photo each time, so that the posts feel fresh. If someone commented with a question, reply quickly, since a reply is often the push a hesitant buyer needs.

Do not run offers all the time. If every post is a discount, customers learn to wait for the next one. A good balance is mostly helpful and human posts, with an offer now and then. ## Checking the post before it goes live

Read the post once as a customer who has never heard of your offer. Can you tell what it is, what it covers, and when it ends? Then read it as the person who will serve that customer. Does the team know about the offer, the limits, and how to handle questions? A short briefing before a sale starts saves many awkward moments at the counter or in the inbox.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **No end date.** A sale with no deadline gives no reason to act now.
- **Burying the offer.** Do not make people read three sentences to find it.
- **Using all capital letters.** It is hard to read and feels like shouting.
- **Unclear limits.** Surprises at the till make customers angry.
- **A broken link.** Test it before you publish.
- **Forgetting to end the post.** After the offer ends, remove or edit the post so no one expects it.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a sale post be?

Two to four short sentences are enough for most offers. Put the key facts first, and use a photo to do some of the work.

### Should I use percentages or dollar amounts?

Use whichever is clearer for your customer. For low-priced items, a dollar amount can feel bigger. For higher prices, a percentage is easier to understand. Show a real example if it helps.

### Can AI write my sale post?

A tool can draft one, but you must supply the true facts and check every detail. Our list of [AI prompts for Facebook posts](/blog/ai-prompts-for-facebook-posts) shows how to do it safely.

### What should I do when the offer ends?

Tell people that it has ended, and thank those who took part. You can also edit the post so no one comes in expecting a discount.
