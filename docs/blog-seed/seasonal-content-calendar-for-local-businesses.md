Your customers do not think about the same things all year. In one month they plan holidays, in another they prepare for school, and in another they stay indoors and look for comfort. A seasonal content calendar for local businesses turns that rhythm into a plan. You decide, once, which moments matter to your customers, and you prepare posts ahead of time instead of scrambling on the day.

This guide shows you how to build one, step by step, with example hooks for each part of the year. The ideas are our own suggestions. Holidays, school terms, and seasons differ by country, so use your own local calendar and treat our examples as prompts, not a schedule. For the monthly planning habit that this builds on, see our guide on [how to plan a month of Facebook content](/blog/how-to-plan-a-month-of-facebook-content).

## Why seasonal planning helps

- **You see busy and quiet periods early.** You can prepare posts, stock, and staff before a rush.
- **You post at the moment customers are thinking about it.** A gardening tip in planting season lands better than in winter.
- **You avoid last-minute stress.** Special dates get a post written weeks ahead.
- **You can reuse the plan.** Next year you start with a tested outline.

Seasonal posts work best when they are useful. A reminder that your shop is open late before a holiday helps people. A picture of a pumpkin does not, unless it connects to something you sell.

## What counts as a season for a local business

A season is any time of year that changes what your customers need. It can be:

- **Weather seasons:** hot, cold, rainy, dry, harvest.
- **National or religious holidays and festivals** that your customers celebrate.
- **School and work calendars:** exams, term starts, summer breaks.
- **Local events:** a market, a fair, a sports final, or a town anniversary.
- **Industry moments:** tax time, wedding season, moving season.
- **Your own dates:** your shop's anniversary, a sale, a new menu.

Make a list for your place. Ask your staff and a few regular customers, because they know what matters locally.

## The four-step method

### Step 1: List the dates

Write every date that could matter to your customers over twelve months. Do not judge yet. Include fixed dates, such as holidays, and flexible ones, such as the first cold week.

### Step 2: Score each date

Mark each date with a number from one to three.

- **3:** Customers clearly change their behavior, and you have something to offer.
- **2:** It matters a little, and you can tie in a simple post.
- **1:** It is on the calendar, but it has little to do with your business.

Plan fully for the threes, lightly for the twos, and skip the ones.

### Step 3: Add the lead time

Decide how far ahead each date needs a post. A big holiday may need a reminder three weeks before, one week before, and one day before. A small tip may need one post.

### Step 4: Write the post types

For each date, choose the kind of post: a tip, an offer, an announcement, a question, or a thank-you. Mix the types so the calendar does not become all offers.

## A year of example hooks

The table shows example hooks for each part of a year. Adjust the months to your climate and your customs.

| Part of the year | What customers may be thinking about | Example hook |
|---|---|---|
| New year | Fresh starts, goals, budgets | "Three small changes to start the year well" |
| Late winter or early spring | Cleaning, planning, renewal | "A checklist for your spring refresh" |
| Spring | Outdoor activity, events, weddings | "Book early for the busy season" |
| Early summer | Heat, travel, school breaks | "Keep cool: our summer opening hours" |
| Mid summer | Holidays, long days | "A quick guide to our holiday closures" |
| Late summer | Back to school or work | "Ready for the new term? Here is our checklist" |
| Autumn | Harvest, comfort, preparing for cold | "Our autumn menu starts Monday" |
| Late autumn | Gifts, deadlines, travel | "Order by this date to get it in time" |
| Winter | Warmth, family, celebrations | "How we are keeping you warm this season" |

These are starting points. Replace them with your own customers' real concerns.

## Examples for different businesses

- **A cafe:** a new seasonal drink, a warm-weather terrace post, a holiday opening-hours reminder.
- **A garden shop:** planting guides, watering tips, a frost warning with advice on protecting plants.
- **A salon:** a style for a wedding season, a back-to-school trim, a holiday booking reminder.
- **A repair shop:** a bike check before cycling season, a heater check before winter.
- **A real estate agent:** a spring-market guide, a note about preparing a home for winter. See our list of [Facebook post ideas for real estate agents](/blog/facebook-post-ideas-for-real-estate-agents).
- **A tutor or school:** exam-season study tips, a term start reminder.

## How to lay out the calendar

Use a simple table with one row per week or one per month. For each row, write:

1. The date or period.
2. The reason customers care.
3. The post type and a short note.
4. The photo you will need.
5. The date you will write it by.

## Mix seasonal posts with everyday ones

A calendar full of holidays can feel thin. Keep a base of everyday posts, such as tips, behind-the-scenes, and customer questions, and let seasonal posts sit on top. A good ratio for many Pages is mostly everyday posts, with seasonal posts at the right moments. Test the balance on your own Page and adjust it.

## Be careful with holidays and events

- **Respect the occasion.** Do not use a serious or sacred day only as a sales hook.
- **Check dates every year.** Some holidays move.
- **Match your audience.** Do not assume everyone celebrates the same days.
- **Do not borrow another brand's slogans or images.** Use your own words and pictures.
- **Keep offers honest.** A "holiday sale" should be a real sale.

## Turning one date into three posts

A single date can carry a short sequence. Take a local holiday that matters to your customers. A first post, two or three weeks before, gives practical information, such as your opening hours or the last day to order. A second post, a week before, shows the product or service in context. A third, on the day or the day before, is a short reminder or a warm greeting. Afterward, a quick thank-you post closes the loop. The same approach works for a slow season: a tip, a question, and a small offer spread over a few weeks. Keep each post different in photo and wording, so the sequence feels useful rather than repetitive.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Planning too late.** A post for a holiday on the day itself is easy to miss.
- **Copying a national calendar blindly.** Local customs and weather differ.
- **All offers.** Seasonal posts can help, teach, and thank.
- **Forgetting to review.** At the end of the year, note what worked and update the plan.
- **Ignoring quiet seasons.** A slow month is a good time for helpful content that builds trust.

## Frequently asked questions

### How far ahead should I plan?

Draft the full year as an outline, then write posts one to three months ahead. Review it each month.

### What if my business has no obvious seasons?

Most do. Look at your own sales and questions by month. You may find quiet patterns, such as more orders after payday or fewer visits during exams.

### Can I reuse last year's posts?

Reuse the idea, not the exact text. Update facts, dates, and photos, and give each post a new first line.

### How do I know the plan is working?

Compare your seasonal posts with your other posts. Our guide to a [Facebook Page content audit](/blog/how-to-do-a-facebook-page-content-audit) shows how.
