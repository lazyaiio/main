If you run a Facebook Page for a business, you already know the feeling. It is Tuesday afternoon, you have not posted all week, and you are staring at a blank box wondering what to say. The fix is not more inspiration. The fix is a plan. When you plan facebook content a month at a time, the daily question changes from "What on earth should I post?" to "What is next on the list?" That one change saves hours and makes your Page feel steadier to the people who follow it.

This guide walks through a simple seven-step method for planning a month of Facebook Page content. It works for a restaurant, a salon, an online shop, a coach, or a one-person service business. You do not need special software. A notebook or a spreadsheet is enough, and by the end you will have a plan you can follow and a routine that keeps it alive.

## Why planning beats posting on impulse

Posting when you feel like it has a hidden cost. Weeks with many posts are followed by weeks with none. Your topics drift. You repeat yourself without noticing, or you forget to mention the one offer that matters. Followers cannot tell what your Page is for, and the people who might have bought from you scroll past.

A plan solves three problems at once:

- **Consistency.** A regular rhythm tells followers that your Page is active and worth following.
- **Balance.** You can mix helpful posts, personal posts, and sales posts on purpose instead of by accident.
- **Time.** Writing several posts in one sitting is faster than writing one post at a time on ten different days, because you stay in the same frame of mind.

Planning also lowers stress. When the month is mapped out, a busy week does not mean a silent Page. You already know what goes out and when.

## Step 1: Decide what your Page is for

Before you plan a single post, write down in one sentence what the Page should do for your business. Be honest and specific. "Get more customers" is too vague. Better examples are "Fill the empty weekday appointments," "Send people to our online shop," or "Keep past customers in touch so they come back."

Then choose one main goal for the month. Most small businesses fit into one of four goals:

1. **Be noticed** by new people who have never heard of you.
2. **Build engagement**, which means comments, shares, and conversations.
3. **Drive sales** through offers, bookings, or orders.
4. **Build trust** so that people feel safe choosing you.

You can serve more than one goal, but the main goal decides the mix of posts. A Page focused on sales needs more offers and proof. A Page focused on being noticed needs more shareable, useful posts. If you are unsure, trust is a good default for small local businesses, because people buy from businesses they trust.

## Step 2: Know who you are talking to

A plan for "everyone" turns into posts for no one. Describe your ideal reader in a few lines: who they are, what problem they want solved, what they worry about, and what they usually ask before buying. If you have been in business for a while, you already have the answers. Look at your last ten customer messages and note what they asked.

These questions become your content. Every question a customer asks is a post waiting to be written. If five people asked about delivery times, a clear post about delivery will help hundreds of people who never asked. ## Step 3: Choose four or five content themes

Themes are the repeating types of posts that make up your plan. They keep you from starting from zero every time. A good set of themes covers different reasons people follow a Page. Here is a set that works for many businesses:

- **Helpful tips**: short advice related to what you sell.
- **Behind the scenes**: how you make, prepare, or deliver your work.
- **Customer stories**: real people and real results, with permission.
- **Offers and news**: what is new, what is on sale, what is opening.
- **Questions and polls**: simple prompts that invite people to answer.

Pick the four or five that fit you best. If a theme feels forced, drop it. You can read more ideas for each type in our list of [Facebook post ideas for small businesses](/blog/facebook-post-ideas-for-small-businesses). A good rule of thumb is that most of your posts should help, entertain, or inform, and a smaller share should ask for a sale. When every post is an advertisement, people stop paying attention.

## Step 4: Set a posting rhythm you can keep

The right number of posts is the number you will actually publish for months, not the number you can manage for one excited week. For many small businesses, two to four posts per week is a realistic start. If that sounds like a lot, begin with two and add more once the routine feels easy.

Pick fixed days. For example, Monday for a tip, Wednesday for a behind-the-scenes post, and Friday for an offer. Fixed days remove decisions, and your regular followers begin to expect your posts. Our guide on [how often to post on Facebook](/blog/how-often-should-you-post-on-facebook) explains how to choose a rhythm and how to adjust it using your own results.

> Tip: It is better to post twice a week for a year than daily for two weeks. Choose a pace that survives your busiest month.

## Step 5: Build the calendar

Now put it all together. Create a table with one row per post and these columns: date, day, theme, topic, format (photo, short video, text, link), and status (idea, drafted, scheduled, published). Fill in the dates first, using the days you chose. Then assign a theme to each date so that the themes rotate. Finally, give every post a topic, even if it is only a few words.

Here is how a simple week might look for a neighborhood bakery:

- **Monday, tip:** How to keep bread fresh for three days.
- **Wednesday, behind the scenes:** The baker shaping loaves at 5 a.m.
- **Friday, offer:** A weekend box of pastries, with the last order time.

Add the dates that matter to your business: holidays, school breaks, a product launch, a local event, or the busy season. Plan the posts that support them first, then fill the remaining spots with your regular themes. Look one month ahead, but leave one or two blank slots for news you cannot predict, such as a sudden sell-out or a happy customer message.

## Step 6: Write and prepare in batches

Batching is where the planning pays off. Instead of writing each post on its day, set aside one session to write the whole week, or the whole month if you can. In that session:

1. Write the post text for each calendar row.
2. Choose or take the photo or short video for each post.
3. Add a clear next step to each post, such as a question, a link, a phone number, or a booking button.
4. Read each post out loud once. If it sounds stiff, shorten it.

Writing in one sitting is faster because you stay in the same mindset, and it lets you step back and check the balance of the month. Look at the whole calendar and ask: is there a good mix? Is there too much selling in one week? Do the photos look varied?

## Step 7: Review and improve every month

A plan is a guess until your audience responds. At the end of the month, spend thirty minutes looking at what happened. Open your Page Insights and note which posts got the most reactions, comments, shares, and clicks. You are not hunting for a magic formula. You are looking for patterns you can repeat:

- Which themes did people respond to most?
- Which days and times seemed to work better for your audience?
- Which posts brought real messages, bookings, or sales?
- Which posts fell flat, and why might that be?

Then adjust. Keep the themes that work, change the ones that do not, and write down one improvement for next month. For example, "More customer stories, fewer generic tips," or "Move the offer post to Thursday." Over a few months, these small changes make your plan fit your audience closely.

## A simple weekly routine to keep it going

A plan only works if you follow it. This routine takes about an hour a week once you are used to it:

1. **Monday, 15 minutes:** Check the week's posts, reply to comments and messages, and swap any post that no longer fits.
2. **Midweek, 10 minutes:** Respond to new comments and see how the week is going.
3. **Friday, 20 minutes:** Note what worked, and add any new ideas to a running list.
4. **Last week of the month, 60 minutes:** Plan and write the next month in a batch.

Keep an "ideas" list somewhere you can always reach, such as a note on your phone. When a customer asks a good question or something funny happens in the shop, add it. You will never face a blank page again.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Planning too much too soon.** Start with two weeks if a month feels heavy. You can extend the plan later.
- **Only selling.** If every post asks for money, followers stop reading. Mix in posts that help and posts that show people.
- **Ignoring comments.** A plan covers posting, but the conversation after a post matters just as much. Reply promptly and kindly.
- **Never changing the plan.** Treat the calendar as a guide. If a big local event happens, post about it.
- **Copying other Pages.** Learn from them, but use your own words, photos, and details. Specific posts about your real business are more believable than general ones.

## Frequently asked questions

### How far ahead should I plan Facebook content?

A month is a good balance for most small businesses. It is far enough to avoid daily stress and close enough that you can still react to real news. If you are new to planning, start with two weeks.

### How many posts should be in a month?

That depends on your rhythm. Two posts per week gives you eight to nine posts a month, and four per week gives you about sixteen to eighteen. Choose the number you can keep up for several months in a row.

### Do I need a special tool to plan content?

No. A spreadsheet or even a paper calendar works. Tools help when you want to save time on writing and scheduling, but the thinking behind the plan, your goal, your audience, and your themes, matters more than the tool.

### What if I run out of ideas?

Use your customers' questions, your best-selling items, your seasonal events, and the themes above. Our [post idea generator](/tools/post-idea-generator) can also give you a fresh set whenever you need it.

### Should I plan content for other platforms at the same time?

You can reuse the same ideas, but adapt the format and length to each platform. Start with Facebook, get the routine working, and then expand.
