Two lines decide most of what happens to a Facebook post: the first line and the last line. The first line decides whether anyone reads on. The last line decides whether anyone does anything afterward. In writing terms, these are the hook and the facebook call to action. Get both right and an ordinary post starts to work harder. Get them wrong and even a useful post can disappear.

This article gives you twenty-five patterns, thirteen for hooks and twelve for calls to action, with examples you can adapt. It also explains how to choose between them and how to avoid the habits that make posts feel pushy. For the bigger picture, read our guide on [how to write Facebook posts that people read](/blog/how-to-write-facebook-posts-people-read).

## What a hook does

A hook is the opening line of a post. Its only job is to make the right reader want the second line. It does not need to be clever. It needs to be clear, specific, and relevant to the person you are talking to. A good hook answers the silent question every reader asks: "Is this for me, and is it worth my time?"

A hook should also be honest. If the opening promises something the post does not deliver, readers will notice, and trust drops. Promise only what you will give.

## Thirteen hook patterns

### 1. The useful promise

Tell readers what they will get. "Three ways to keep your plants alive in the heat." It works well for tips and how-to posts.

### 2. The direct question

Ask something your reader cares about. "Do you know what is really in your morning coffee?" Choose questions that are easy to answer in your head.

### 3. The specific number

Numbers make a claim concrete. "We baked 40 rolls this morning, and 31 are already gone." Use real numbers only.

### 4. The small story

Start in the middle of a moment. "Last Friday, a customer walked in with a broken watch and one question." Stories draw people in because they want to know what happened.

### 5. The common mistake

Name an error people make. "The biggest mistake we see in small gardens is also the easiest to fix."

### 6. The surprising fact

Share something unexpected about your field. Make sure it is true and be ready to explain it.

### 7. The "you" statement

Speak to a problem. "If your mornings are chaos, this tip is for you." It signals who the post is for.

### 8. The before and after

Show a change. "Before: a cracked screen and a frustrated owner. After: back in his pocket in 30 minutes."

### 9. The myth buster

State a belief, then correct it. "Myth: you need to wash your hair daily. Here is the truth."

### 10. The plain announcement

Sometimes the news itself is the hook. "We are open on Sundays from now on." Keep it short and clear.

### 11. The invitation

Start with a welcome. "Come and meet the team this Saturday." It suits events and openings.

### 12. The confession

Honesty stands out. "We got this wrong last month, and here is what we changed."

### 13. The list teaser

Promise a list. "Five questions to ask before you hire a painter." Lists feel easy to read and easy to save.

> Tip: Write three hooks for each post and choose the strongest. Read them aloud. The best one usually sounds like something you would say to a friend.

## Hooks to avoid

- **Empty excitement.** "We are thrilled to announce" tells the reader nothing and is about you.
- **Clickbait.** "You will not believe what happened" followed by something ordinary teaches readers to ignore you.
- **Vague greetings.** "Hello everyone" wastes the most valuable line.
- **All capital letters.** They are hard to read and feel like shouting.

## What a call to action does

A call to action, often shortened to CTA, tells the reader the next step. Without one, even a post that people enjoyed has nowhere to go. A good call to action is specific, easy, and matched to what the post offers. It also asks for one thing at a time, because a reader faced with three requests usually does none of them.

Think about the effort you are asking for. Commenting costs almost nothing. Messaging costs a little more. Visiting a shop or placing an order costs the most. Match the size of the ask to how warm the relationship is and how good the post is.

## Twelve call-to-action patterns

### 14. The simple question

"Which flavor should we make next? Tell us in the comments." Easy to answer and good for starting a conversation.

### 15. The tag-a-friend

"Tag someone who needs this tip." It can extend your reach, but use it only when the post is truly useful.

### 16. The message invitation

"Send us a message to book your spot." Clear, personal, and good for services.

### 17. The visit invitation

"Come by before noon and try it fresh." Include the address or hours if they are not obvious.

### 18. The link click

"Tap the link to see the full menu." Make sure the link goes exactly where you say and works on a phone.

### 19. The save-this

"Save this checklist for later." It suits tips, recipes, and lists.

### 20. The share

"Share this with anyone planning a trip this summer." Ask only when sharing helps the reader's friends.

### 21. The limited-time offer

"Order by Friday and we will deliver on Saturday." A real deadline helps. A fake one damages trust.

### 22. The booking prompt

"Three appointments left this week. Book yours today." Say the real number and the real deadline.

### 23. The reply prompt

"Reply with the word MENU and we will send it to you." It makes the next step very small.

### 24. The review request

"If we helped you, a short review means a lot." Ask at the right moment, such as after a happy customer message, and never offer rewards that break platform rules.

### 25. The follow prompt

"Follow our Page for a new tip every Tuesday." Tell people what they will get.

## How to choose the right call to action

Ask what you want most from this post, and write the call to action for that. If the aim is conversation, ask a question. If the aim is bookings, invite a message. If the aim is sales, give a clear link or order method. Here is a simple guide:

- **Be noticed:** share, tag a friend, follow.
- **Build engagement:** question, poll, reply prompt.
- **Drive sales:** message, book, order, visit.
- **Build trust:** save this, read more, review request.

Place the call to action at the end of the post, where readers expect it, and use a short sentence. If the post is long, you can also repeat it in a gentler form near the middle.

## Making calls to action feel natural

- **Be specific.** "Message us to book" beats "Contact us."
- **Be polite.** Invite rather than command. Skip pushy phrases like "Do not miss out."
- **Be honest.** Do not create false urgency or hide the price.
- **Make it easy.** Give the phone number, the link, or the exact word to reply with.
- **Follow up.** If someone comments or messages, reply quickly. The call to action works only if you answer.

## Putting it together: an example

Here is a post for a small bike repair shop that uses a story hook and a message call to action:

> "Last week, a customer brought in a bike that had been in a shed for three years. It had flat tires, a rusty chain, and a very hopeful owner. Two hours later, it rode like new. If you have a bike waiting in your garage, send us a message with a photo and we will tell you what it needs, free of charge."

The hook is a small story. The post is short, concrete, and kind. The call to action is a single clear step that costs the reader very little.

## Common mistakes

- **No call to action at all.** Even a gentle question helps.
- **Too many calls to action.** Choose one.
- **A call to action that does not match the post.** Do not end a helpful tip with "Buy now."
- **Asking for likes and shares in a begging tone.** It feels desperate and can reduce trust.
- **Forgetting to check the link.** Test every link on a phone before you publish.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does every post need a call to action?

Most posts benefit from one, even a small one such as a question. Occasionally a post can simply share a moment, and that is fine. Over a month, make sure most posts give people something to do.

### What is the best call to action for engagement?

A simple question that is easy to answer usually works best, especially one that asks for an opinion or a personal choice. Then reply to the comments you receive.

### How many hooks should I test?

Write three for each post and keep the best. Over time, note which styles get better responses from your audience and use more of them.

### Is it okay to use the same call to action every time?

A familiar one can work, but vary it. If every post ends with the same sentence, readers stop noticing it.
