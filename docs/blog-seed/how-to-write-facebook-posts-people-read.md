Most Facebook posts are scrolled past in a second. People move quickly through their feed on a phone, with one thumb, often while doing something else. If your post does not give them a reason to stop, they will never know how good the rest of it was. Learning how to write facebook posts that people actually read is less about clever wording and more about a few simple habits: a strong first line, one clear idea, short and friendly writing, and a next step.

This guide covers those habits one by one, with examples and a checklist you can use before you publish. It is written for business owners and the people who manage their Pages, not for professional copywriters, so every tip is something you can use today.

## Start by knowing who you are writing for

Good writing begins before the first word. Picture one real person who follows your Page: a regular customer, a neighbor, a past client. Think about what they want, what worries them, and what they would ask you if you were talking face to face. Then write to that one person, using the words they would use.

This one change makes posts warmer and clearer. Writing to "our valued customers" sounds like an advertisement. Writing to "you" sounds like a person. Use "you" and "we" freely, and prefer everyday words to business words. If you would not say it out loud to a customer, do not write it.

## Give every post one job

Before you write, finish this sentence: "After reading this post, I want the reader to ___." Fill the blank with one thing: learn a tip, feel welcome, answer a question, visit the shop, or place an order. One post, one job.

Posts that try to do everything usually do nothing. A post that announces a sale, tells your life story, asks for reviews, and promotes a new product leaves the reader unsure what to do. Split it into separate posts. If you are planning many posts at once, our guide to [planning a month of Facebook content](/blog/how-to-plan-a-month-of-facebook-content) shows how to give each post its own purpose.

## Write a first line that earns the second

The first line is the most important line you write. On a busy feed, it may be the only line most people read, and it decides whether they read the rest. A strong first line does one of these things:

- **Promises something useful:** "Three ways to keep bread fresh for a whole week."
- **Asks a question your reader cares about:** "Is your garden ready for the rainy season?"
- **States a surprising or specific fact:** "We sold out of this cake in 40 minutes yesterday."
- **Speaks to a feeling or a problem:** "If mornings are chaos at your house, this is for you."
- **Starts a short story:** "Last Friday, a customer walked in with a broken watch and a big problem."

Avoid starting with "We are excited to announce" or with your business name and a greeting. Those openings are about you, not the reader. Instead, begin with what is in it for them. We cover first lines and calls to action in more detail in our guide to [Facebook post hooks and calls to action](/blog/facebook-post-hooks-and-calls-to-action).

> Tip: Write three different first lines for every post, then keep the best one. It takes a minute and often doubles the quality of the post.

## Write for a phone screen

Most people read Facebook on a phone. That has real consequences for how you write:

- **Short sentences.** Aim for sentences of about ten to twenty words. If a sentence has more than one idea, split it.
- **Short paragraphs.** One to three sentences per paragraph. White space makes text feel easier to read.
- **Simple words.** Use "buy" instead of "purchase," and "help" instead of "assist."
- **Lists for steps.** If a post explains several steps or items, use a numbered or bulleted list.
- **Front-load the point.** Put the most important information at the start, not at the end.

If you are not sure whether a post is easy to read, try reading it aloud. Anywhere you run out of breath or stumble, the sentence is too long. You can also paste your draft into our free [Facebook post checker](/tools/post-checker), which measures sentence length and reading ease.

## Use real details

Specific posts are more believable than general ones. Compare these two sentences:

- "We offer high-quality products at great prices."
- "Our sourdough loaf is baked at 4 a.m. and costs 6 dollars."

The second one tells the reader something real. Wherever you can, add a name, a number, a place, a time, or a small story. Mention the street where your shop is. Say how long the job took. Quote a customer, with permission. Details show that a real business with real people is behind the Page, and that builds trust.

Avoid claims you cannot support, such as "the best in town" or "guaranteed results," unless you can back them up. Honest, specific language is more convincing than big promises, and it keeps you on the right side of advertising rules.

## Choose the right tone

Your tone is how you sound. For most small businesses, a friendly, clear, and respectful tone works well, like a helpful shop assistant speaking to a neighbor. Match your tone to your brand: a children's toy store can be playful, a law office should be calm and precise, and a coffee shop can be relaxed.

Be consistent. If your posts sound cheerful one day and stiff the next, readers will feel the difference. A short list of three or four words describing your voice, such as "warm, direct, practical," helps everyone who writes for your Page.

A few guidelines for any tone:

- Be kind and respectful, even when you disagree.
- Avoid sarcasm that can be misread.
- Use humor carefully and only when it fits your brand.
- Reply to comments in the same voice as your posts.

## Add a clear next step

Every post should tell the reader what to do next, even if the next step is small. Without a next step, a good post ends in silence. Examples of clear next steps:

- **Engage:** "Tell us your favorite flavor in the comments."
- **Visit:** "Come by before noon to try it."
- **Message:** "Send us a message to book your spot."
- **Click:** "Tap the link to see the full menu."
- **Share:** "Tag a friend who needs this tip."

Use one next step per post, and write it as a direct invitation. Avoid forcing it: "Like and share or you will miss out" feels pushy. A calm, specific request works better. You can learn more patterns in our list of [Facebook post hooks and calls to action](/blog/facebook-post-hooks-and-calls-to-action).

## Use photos and video to support the words

A clear image helps a post stand out and gives readers a quick sense of what it is about. Use pictures that show your real business: your products, your place, your people. A bright, steady photo taken with a phone is usually enough. Make sure that the image matches the message, and that any text on the image is large enough to read on a small screen.

Add alternative text to images where the platform allows it. It helps people who use screen readers, and it is simply good practice.

## A before-and-after example

Here is a weak post for a small bakery:

> "We are excited to announce that we now have new products available in our store. Please come and visit us to see our wide range of items at affordable prices. Thank you for your continued support."

It is vague, it is about the business, and it has no real detail or clear next step. Here is a stronger version:

> "Warm cinnamon rolls, out of the oven at 8 a.m. every Saturday. We bake 40, and last week they were gone by 10. Want one saved for you? Message us your name and we will put it aside."

The second post has a clear promise, real numbers, a small story, and a specific next step. It is also shorter and easier to read.

## A checklist to use before you publish

Read your draft and check each item:

1. Does the first line give the reader a reason to keep reading?
2. Does the post have one clear job?
3. Are the sentences short and the words simple?
4. Are there real details: a name, a number, a place?
5. Is there one clear next step?
6. Is the tone friendly and consistent with your brand?
7. Are the spelling, prices, dates, and links correct?
8. Does the photo match the message?
9. Would you be comfortable if a customer read this out loud in your shop?

If you plan and schedule posts in advance, as described in our guide to [scheduling Facebook Page posts](/blog/how-to-schedule-facebook-page-posts), you also have time to read each post a second time with fresh eyes.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Writing like a press release.** Plain, friendly language beats formal language.
- **Burying the point.** Put the most important information first.
- **Long blocks of text.** Break the text into short paragraphs.
- **Too many hashtags and emojis.** A few can help. Many make a post harder to read.
- **Too many links.** One clear link is easier to follow than several.
- **Ignoring comments.** Writing the post is half the job. Reply to people kindly and quickly.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a Facebook post be?

As long as it needs to be and no longer. Short posts of a few lines work well for questions and quick news. Longer posts can work for stories and how-tos if the first line is strong and the text is easy to scan. Test what your audience reads.

### Should I use emojis?

A few well-chosen emojis can add warmth and break up text. Keep them relevant and use them sparingly, especially if your brand is formal.

### Should I use hashtags on Facebook?

A small number of relevant hashtags, such as two or three, is plenty. They are less central on Facebook than on some other platforms, so do not let them crowd the post.

### How do I find the right words if I am not a writer?

Write the way you speak, then edit. Start by explaining the post to a friend, write down what you said, and tighten it. Tools can help with drafts, and you can always change the words to sound like you.

### Can AI help me write posts?

Yes, it can save time on first drafts, but you should always read and edit the result. See our guide on [using AI to write Facebook posts](/blog/how-to-use-ai-to-write-facebook-posts) for how to do it well.
