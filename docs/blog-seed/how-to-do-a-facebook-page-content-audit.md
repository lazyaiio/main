After a few months of posting, most Pages run on habit. You post what you posted last week, and you hope it is working. A Facebook Page content audit stops that cycle. It is a structured look at what you have published, what people did with it, and what you should do more or less of. You can finish a useful one in a single afternoon, and it needs no special software.

This guide gives you a step-by-step method, a worksheet you can copy, and the questions to ask at each stage. The method is our own, built on common practice. Numbers on your own Page matter more than any general advice, which is why the audit looks at your data rather than at averages. For the planning habit that comes after the audit, see our guide on [how to plan a month of Facebook content](/blog/how-to-plan-a-month-of-facebook-content).

## What a content audit is, and is not

A content audit is a review of your past posts. It answers three questions:

1. What did we publish?
2. How did people respond?
3. What will we change?

It is not a judgment of your worth, and it is not about chasing big numbers. A small Page with steady, relevant posts can be healthy. The aim is to find patterns you can use.

## Before you start: set the scope

Decide how much to review.

- **Time period:** the last three months is a good start. Six is fine if you post rarely.
- **Number of posts:** most Pages can review thirty to sixty posts in an afternoon.
- **Goal:** pick one main goal, such as more messages, more visits to your site, or more bookings.

A clear goal tells you which numbers matter. If your aim is bookings, then comments asking for prices tell you more than a count of likes.

## Step 1: Collect your posts

Make a simple table, in a spreadsheet or on paper. Create one row per post and add these columns:

| Column | What to write |
|---|---|
| Date | When it went live |
| Type | Tip, offer, behind the scenes, question, announcement, other |
| Topic | A few words on the subject |
| Format | Photo, video, link, text only |
| Reach | The number of people who saw it, if you can see it |
| Reactions, comments, shares | Counts from the post |
| Clicks or messages | If you track them |
| Notes | Anything that stands out |

Where you can find these numbers depends on the tools you use. Meta's Page tools show some, and a scheduling tool may show others. Our guide to [Facebook Page metrics explained](/blog/facebook-page-metrics-explained) describes what each number means.

## Step 2: Look at the mix

Count how many posts you made of each type and format. Then ask:

- Are most posts the same type? A Page that is all offers, or all photos, may be too narrow.
- Does the mix match your goal? If you want conversation, are there question posts?
- Were there long gaps? A month with no posts is worth noting.
- Does the rhythm match what you can keep? Our guide on [how often to post on Facebook](/blog/how-often-should-you-post-on-facebook) can help.

Write down what you notice, without judging it yet.

## Step 3: Find your best and weakest posts

Sort the table by the number that matters most for your goal, such as comments, clicks, or messages. Mark the top five and the bottom five.

Then compare them honestly. Ask what the top posts have in common:

- A type, such as questions or tips?
- A topic, such as a particular product?
- A format, such as photos of people?
- A time of day or a day of the week?
- A strong first line or a clear next step?

Do the same for the bottom five. A pattern needs at least three examples before you trust it, and even then it is a hint, not a rule. Our guide on [how to calculate engagement rate](/blog/how-to-calculate-engagement-rate-on-facebook) can help you compare posts fairly when their reach differs. ## Step 4: Check the quality of each post

Numbers tell you what happened, not why. For the top and bottom posts, read the actual text and look at the photo. Ask:

1. Does the first line make sense on its own?
2. Is there one clear next step?
3. Is the photo clear, well lit, and relevant?
4. Is the length right for the point? See our guide on [how long a Facebook post should be](/blog/how-long-should-a-facebook-post-be).
5. Are the facts accurate and up to date?
6. Does it sound like your business?

You can run a few drafts through the free [Facebook post checker](/tools/post-checker) for a quick second opinion on the first line and the call to action.

## Step 5: Check the conversation

Read the comments and messages on your last twenty posts. Make a note of:

- Questions people asked more than once.
- Complaints or confusion.
- Replies you missed.
- Praise you could thank people for.

Repeated questions are post ideas. Missed replies are a habit to fix. Our guide on [replying to Facebook comments and reviews](/blog/how-to-reply-to-facebook-comments-and-reviews) can help you build a routine.

## Step 6: Check the basics of the Page

Spend ten minutes on the Page itself.

- **Name and category:** Do they describe what you do?
- **About section:** Are the hours, address, phone number, and website correct?
- **Profile and cover photos:** Are they current and clear on a phone?
- **Pinned or featured post:** Does it still apply?
- **Buttons and links:** Do they work?
- **Services or products listed:** Are they up to date?

Wrong basics undo good posts, because people cannot find what they need.

## Step 7: Decide what to do more of, less of, and differently

Now turn your notes into a short plan. Use three lists.

- **Do more of:** the types, topics, and formats that worked.
- **Do less of:** the ones that took effort and got little back.
- **Try:** two or three experiments, such as a new post type or a different day.

Keep the plan small. Three changes you will make beat ten you will forget.

## How often to repeat it

A full audit every three months is enough for most small Pages. In between, a ten-minute check once a month is useful: look at your best and worst post and ask why. Add the results to your notes so that you build up a picture over time.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Chasing vanity numbers.** Pick the numbers that match your goal.
- **Drawing big conclusions from tiny samples.** One lucky post is not a rule.
- **Comparing with other Pages.** Your audience is your own.
- **Skipping the action plan.** An audit without changes is only a report.
- **Ignoring the basics.** Check hours and links first.
- **Judging a post too soon.** Give a post a few days before you count its results.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does an audit take?

About two to three hours for a small Page, including writing the plan. The first one takes longest.

### Do I need paid tools?

No. A spreadsheet and the numbers that Facebook and your scheduling tool already show are enough.

### What if I have too few posts?

Review what you have and plan an experiment. A month of steady posting will give you more to learn from next time.

### Should I delete weak posts?

Usually not. Delete only posts that are wrong, out of date, or against your standards. Learn from the rest.
