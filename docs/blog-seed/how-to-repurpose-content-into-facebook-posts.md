Most small businesses do not have a content shortage. They have a time shortage. You write one helpful article, a guide, or a newsletter, and then you start from a blank page again for Facebook. There is a faster way. You can repurpose content into Facebook posts, which means taking one piece of work you already did and turning it into several small, useful posts.

This guide explains the method in plain steps, shows a worked example, and warns you about the mistakes that make repurposed posts feel lazy. The approach is our own and it needs no special tools. For the wider planning habit that this fits into, see our guide on [how to plan a month of Facebook content](/blog/how-to-plan-a-month-of-facebook-content).

## What repurposing means (and what it does not)

Repurposing does not mean pasting the same text five times. It means finding the separate ideas inside one piece of content, and giving each idea its own post. One article might hold a tip, a mistake to avoid, a short story, a question, and a link to the full text. Those are five different posts that each stand on their own.

It also does not mean that every follower sees every post. Many of your followers will see only some of what you share, so a point can be worth repeating in a fresh way after a few weeks. The key word is fresh. A new angle, a new photo, and a new first line make the same idea feel new.

## Why it works for small teams

- **It saves thinking time.** The hard thinking is already done in the source.
- **It keeps your message consistent.** All the posts come from one point of view.
- **It leads people to your best work.** A short post can link to the full guide.
- **It fits a calendar.** You can spread one source across a week and move on.

## The source material you can use

You can repurpose almost anything that you made with care:

- A blog article or guide.
- A newsletter or a long email you sent to customers.
- A video, a talk, or a webinar recording.
- A customer story or a case study.
- A frequently asked questions page.
- A product page with a clear list of benefits.
- A set of answers you gave to customers in messages, with names removed.

Choose something that is useful, accurate, and still true. Old advice that has changed is not worth repeating.

## The five-post method

Take one source and pull out five kinds of posts. The table shows each one with its job.

| Post | What to take from the source | Its job |
|---|---|---|
| The tip | The single most useful point | Help the reader today |
| The mistake | A common error and how to avoid it | Build trust |
| The story | A short example or a customer moment | Make it human |
| The question | One question the topic raises | Start a conversation |
| The link | A short summary and the full guide | Send readers to the whole piece |

Write each post so it makes sense without the others. A reader who sees only the mistake post should still understand it and benefit from it.

## A worked example

Here is an example, not real results. A small bakery wrote a guide called "How to keep bread fresh for a week." Here is how it becomes five posts.

1. **The tip (Monday).** "Store a cut loaf cut-side down on a board. It keeps the inside softer for longer." A photo of the loaf on a board.
2. **The mistake (Tuesday).** "The most common bread mistake we see is keeping it in the fridge. Here is why it dries out faster there." A short explanation.
3. **The story (Thursday).** A short note about a customer who asked how to keep a loaf for a family weekend, and what the bakery suggested.
4. **The question (Friday).** "How do you store your bread: a bag, a box, or a board? Tell us and we will share the most popular answer."
5. **The link (Sunday).** "Our full guide to keeping bread fresh is on our website. Here are the four rules in short." A link to the guide.

Notice that no two posts say the same sentence. Each one starts in a different place and asks the reader to do something different.

## How to do it in about an hour

1. **Pick the source.** Choose one piece with at least three separate ideas.
2. **Highlight the ideas.** Mark each distinct point, example, or question.
3. **Match each idea to a post type.** Use the table above.
4. **Write a new first line for each post.** Use the patterns in our guide to [hooks and calls to action](/blog/facebook-post-hooks-and-calls-to-action).
5. **Add one photo or graphic for each post.** A different image each time keeps your Page fresh.
6. **Add one clear next step.** A question, a link, or an invitation to message you.
7. **Schedule them across the week.** Spread them out rather than posting them together.

## Adapting the same idea for different goals

The same source can serve different goals depending on the call to action.

- **For awareness:** ask a question or invite a share.
- **For trust:** give a tip that costs the reader nothing.
- **For visits to your site:** link to the guide and say what is inside.
- **For sales:** tie the tip to a product that solves the problem, once, and honestly.

Do not turn every post into a sales message. If one in five posts sells something and the rest are useful, the sales post lands better.

## How to tell whether it is working

Look at your Page after a month. Compare the repurposed posts with your other posts. Which ones got more replies, messages, or clicks to your website? There is no universal benchmark, so judge by your own Page. If the tip posts do best, write more tips. If the question posts get answers, ask more questions. Keep a short note of what you learn, and your next batch gets easier to plan.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Copying whole paragraphs.** Rewrite for a short post. Readers on a phone want a few clear lines.
- **Posting all five on one day.** Spread them out so each has room.
- **Repeating the same photo.** Use new images, or crop the same scene in a new way.
- **Forgetting to check facts.** If the source is old, update numbers, dates, and prices first.
- **Skipping replies.** The question post only works if you answer the comments.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many posts can I make from one article?

Most articles hold three to seven separate ideas. Start with five and stop when an idea would repeat what you already said.

### Is it okay to repost the same idea later?

Yes, if you give it a new angle and a new first line. Wait a few weeks, and check that the advice still holds.

### Do I need an AI tool to repurpose content?

No. The method works with a pen and a document. If you do use AI to draft the short versions, read and edit every post. Our guide on [using AI to write Facebook posts](/blog/how-to-use-ai-to-write-facebook-posts) explains how to keep the writing sounding like you.

### Should the posts link to the original?

Link in one or two of the five, not all of them. Posts that only point elsewhere can feel thin.

Choose one article, video, or customer story you already have. Mark five separate ideas in it and write one short post for each. Then put them on your calendar for next week.
